Preventive Strategy

The Executive Protection Approach to Preventive Dental Care

📅 June 18, 2026
✎ Jack Stromgren, DDS
⌚ 7 min read

In close-protection work, advance work is the part nobody sees. Someone walks the route, checks the exits, and finds the problem before the principal arrives. Jack Stromgren built the first pillar of the Executive Protection Dental Plan on the same premise: preventive dentistry is reconnaissance, and most coverage treats it as paperwork.

Stromgren, a restorative and implant dentist in Willow Glen, San José, has run an independent practice there for twelve years. The pattern he describes is consistent. Patients arrive with a problem that was visible two years earlier and was not acted on, usually because nothing in their coverage made acting on it worthwhile. Advance Work is the pillar that tries to remove that incentive.

What Advance Work Actually Means

The protection version of advance work is specific. Before a principal moves, someone has already driven the route, identified the nearest hospital, and noted which doors lock from the inside. The work is boring by design. Its value is measured in events that do not happen, which makes it the first thing cut when a budget tightens.

Translated into dentistry, advance work is the diagnostic layer: imaging, periodontal charting, occlusal assessment, and the unhurried examination that finds a cracked cusp before it becomes a root fracture. Stromgren narrates every procedure before performing it, a habit that began as a response to patients with severe dental anxiety and turned out to serve the same function advance work serves. People act on information they understand.

The failure point is always the thing nobody planned for. In dentistry it is almost never the tooth that hurts.

Why Conventional Coverage Underweights Prevention

Most dental plans are structured to contain cost. That is not a criticism of the people who administer them; it is a description of what they were built to do. Annual maximums, waiting periods, and frequency limits are cost-control instruments, and they apply to diagnosis as readily as to treatment.

The consequence is that the cheapest part of dental care, the part that prevents the expensive part, gets rationed alongside everything else. The argument Stromgren has developed across twelve years at the Willow Glen practice he runs is that rationing diagnosis to save money is the same mistake as skipping the route survey to save an hour.

What This Looks Like Downstream

Advance Work comes first because the other three pillars depend on it. If diagnosis is thorough, rapid response dental coverage is called on less often, and when it is called on the problem is already documented. If findings are complete, referrals into the plan’s vetted specialist network arrive with a clinical picture attached rather than a complaint and a date.

There is a limit to how far the analogy travels, and Stromgren is careful about it. Advance work in protection assumes a known route. Dentistry deals with biology, which does not publish its itinerary. The pillar is a posture toward diagnosis, not a promise that nothing will go wrong. The practice keeps an ongoing record of its work on the practice’s Instagram feed.

Conclusion

Advance Work is the least dramatic of the four pillars and the one Stromgren returns to most often. It generates no urgency, which is why conventional coverage discounts it, and it is the reason the other three get used less. A plan that funds finding things is a plan that spends less fixing them.
