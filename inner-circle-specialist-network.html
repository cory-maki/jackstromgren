The Inner Circle: How a Vetted Specialist Network Works

📅 August 20, 2026
✎ Jack Stromgren, DDS
⌚ 7 min read

Protection details do not assemble their team during an incident. The roster exists first, vetted, rehearsed, and known. The Inner Circle, the third pillar of the Jack Stromgren Executive Protection Dental Plan, applies that sequence to specialist referral, which in conventional dental coverage is usually a directory lookup performed under pressure.

Stromgren, a restorative and implant dentist practicing on Lincoln Avenue in Willow Glen, San José, spent three years in dedicated continuing education before he considered himself qualified to present implant work as a service of his practice. That standard, applied to himself, is the standard this pillar applies to everyone else on the roster.

What a Directory Is Not

An insurance directory answers one question: who has agreed to the fee schedule. It does not record how many of a given procedure a clinician performs in a year, whether they return calls from referring offices, or whether their surgical planning is legible to the dentist who will restore the case. Those are the things that determine whether a referral helps the patient.

The Inner Circle is a curated roster rather than a list of participating providers. The vetting criteria are the ones a referring clinician actually uses: case volume in the relevant procedure, quality of communication back to the referring office, and willingness to be reached when something goes sideways.

A directory tells you who accepts the fee schedule. It does not tell you who answers the phone on a Saturday.

Why Vetting Has to Happen Early

Referral quality degrades under time pressure. A patient who needs an oral surgeon today gets whoever is available today, and availability correlates poorly with fit. Building the roster in advance removes the decision from the moment of need, which is the same logic that governs advance work as preventive strategy.

It also makes the second pillar coherent. 24-hour emergency dental access is only meaningful if there is somewhere for the patient to go at hour three. A standing roster is what converts a response time into a treatment plan.

The Referring Clinician’s Side of It

There is a detail about specialist referral that patients rarely see: the referring dentist carries the case afterward. Restorative work follows implant placement. Occlusal management follows orthodontics. If the specialist’s planning does not account for the restoration that comes next, the referring clinician inherits the problem, and so does the patient.

That is why the pillar is written from the referring side rather than the patient’s. Its criteria reflect twelve years of receiving cases back, well and badly planned, at the practice run by the clinician who designed the plan. Conclusion

The Inner Circle is the pillar hardest to fake, because a roster either exists before it is needed or it does not. It cannot be assembled during an emergency and it cannot be substituted with a directory. For a plan built on the premise that the danger you do not see is the one that gets you, having the specialist already vetted is not a convenience feature. It is the point.
