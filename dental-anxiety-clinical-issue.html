Why Dental Anxiety Is a Clinical Issue, Not a Personality Quirk

📅 July 14, 2026
✎ Jack Stromgren, DDS
⌚ 6 min read

Avoidance is a clinical variable, not a personality trait. In a Willow Glen practice where a substantial share of the adult caseload has not seen a dentist in a decade, Jack Stromgren treats dental anxiety as information about what treatment is actually possible.

Patients describe it as embarrassment. Charts describe it as a failed recall history. Neither framing is much use at the chair. What matters clinically is that a patient who cannot sit through an examination will not receive one, and a patient who does not receive an examination accumulates disease that becomes more invasive to treat with every year of delay. Jack Stromgren, who has practiced general dentistry on Lincoln Avenue in San José for twelve years, sees the consequence of that arithmetic several times a week: a first visit at forty-five for a problem that was treatable at thirty-two.

What avoidance costs, in clinical terms

The progression is predictable. A small carious lesion that would have taken twenty minutes and a composite becomes a crown, then endodontic treatment, then an extraction and an implant. Periodontal disease a hygienist could have arrested with scaling becomes bone loss that no amount of maintenance will reverse. None of this is unusual, and none of it requires unusual neglect. It requires only a decade of not going.

Anxious patients also present with findings that track the anxiety itself. Attrition and cracked cusps from bruxism. Cervical abrasion from aggressive brushing done in place of professional care. Teeth restored repeatedly at emergency visits and never sequenced into a plan, because every appointment was a crisis and no appointment was a consultation. Sorting that out takes a longer first visit than a standard examination slot allows.

A patient who cannot sit through an examination will not receive one, and the disease does not pause while the fear is discussed.

Why narration changes the appointment

The practice protocol is to describe every step before performing it. Not in general terms at the start of the appointment, but immediately before each action: what instrument is coming, what it will feel like, how long it will take, and what the stopping signal is. This is not a comfort measure bolted onto treatment. It removes the specific thing that drives most dental fear, which is not pain but the absence of any control over what happens next.

The second half of the protocol is time. Appointments are scheduled long enough to absorb pauses, and a patient who needs to stop does not cost the schedule its next three slots. That has a downstream effect on capacity: Stromgren's office books about eleven weeks out, and most of the demand arrives by referral from patients who had already been through the high-volume version. Some of those referrals start in his Reddit discussions about what to expect from a first appointment.

Anxiety is a treatment-planning input

Once fear is treated as data, it changes sequencing. A patient who can tolerate one hour but not two gets a plan built in one-hour units. A patient whose gag reflex makes impressions unworkable gets scanning instead. A patient who cannot tolerate CPAP for much the same reason they cannot tolerate a rubber dam may be a better candidate for oral appliance therapy for sleep apnea, and that decision belongs in the treatment plan rather than in a referral letter.

It also changes what gets promised. Nobody is told a procedure will be painless, because that claim gets tested within ninety seconds and fails. The office describes what is likely, what is possible, and what the patient can do about it, which is the same standard applied to restorative and implant treatment planning elsewhere in the practice. Notes on how first visits are structured also go out in his Threads updates.

Conclusion

Dental anxiety is not a marketing category. It is a clinical variable that determines what a patient will accept, what a clinician can deliver, and how much disease accumulates in between. Treating it as a personality quirk produces charts full of failed recalls. Treating it as a finding produces treatment plans patients actually complete, which is the only measure that means anything twelve years into a practice in Willow Glen.
