What Oral Appliance Therapy for Sleep Apnea Actually Involves

📅 August 11, 2026
✎ Jack Stromgren, DDS
⌚ 7 min read

Oral appliance therapy is a medically recognized alternative to CPAP for some patients with obstructive sleep apnea, and it is a dental treatment with a dental workflow. Jack Stromgren, DDS, whose Willow Glen practice in San José added the service after a single CPAP-intolerant patient, sets out what the process involves.

The service started with one case. A patient with a confirmed diagnosis and a machine he could not use asked whether there was anything else, and the answer required several months of reading, a course, and a conversation with the physician who had made the diagnosis. Jack Stromgren now sees appliance cases regularly at the Lincoln Avenue office, and the sequence has not changed much since that first one: confirm the diagnosis, confirm candidacy, take records, fit, titrate, verify.

It starts with a diagnosis somebody else made

A dentist does not diagnose obstructive sleep apnea. A sleep study does, read by a physician, and the resulting report gives an apnea-hypopnea index and an oxygen desaturation profile that determine whether an appliance is a reasonable option at all. Patients who arrive at a dental office with a suspicion and no study get referred for one. Patients who arrive with a study and a CPAP machine they have abandoned are in a different position, because CPAP intolerance is itself a documented clinical indication.

Candidacy also depends on the mouth. An appliance advances the mandible and holds it there for seven or eight hours, which requires enough sound teeth to anchor to, a temporomandibular joint that tolerates protrusion, and periodontal support that will not be damaged by the load. That assessment is a restorative examination, and patients who need crown and bridge work first are sequenced that way rather than fitted around the problem.

An appliance does not treat apnea by existing. It treats apnea at a specific millimetre of advancement, verified by a second sleep study.

Records, fitting, and titration

Records are a set of impressions or scans, a bite registration taken at a protruded position, and a measurement of maximum comfortable protrusion. The appliance that comes back from the laboratory is adjustable, which is the point. Patients are fitted at a conservative starting position, usually well short of where the appliance will end up, because one advanced too far too fast produces jaw soreness and ends up in the same drawer as the CPAP machine.

Titration is then a matter of weeks. Small advancements, a few tenths of a millimetre at a time, with the patient reporting on snoring, witnessed apneas, morning headaches, and daytime sleepiness. Once symptoms plateau, the physician orders a follow-up study with the appliance in place, and the numbers either confirm the therapy or they do not. Recordings that walk through the titration sequence are on his clinical video library on Vimeo.

Side effects, follow-up, and who it suits

The common side effects are manageable and mostly early: excess salivation, transient jaw stiffness, tooth tenderness. The one that matters long term is occlusal change, since years of nightly mandibular advancement can alter the bite measurably. That is a reason for annual review by the dentist who fitted the appliance rather than a reason to avoid therapy, and it is one argument for keeping restorative work and appliance therapy in the same office. Questions about alternatives to a machine get answered in his Quora answers on CPAP.

Appliance therapy suits patients with mild to moderate obstructive sleep apnea, patients with severe disease who cannot tolerate CPAP, and patients whose sleeping arrangements make a machine impractical. It suits apprehensive patients for a related reason: a device the patient controls is easier to accept than one that pushes air, which is the same logic behind treating dental anxiety as a clinical finding. Candidacy assessments across Santa Clara County start with the restorative examination described in the practice's clinical focus.

Conclusion

Oral appliance therapy is neither a gadget nor a substitute for diagnosis. It is a dental treatment with records, a fitting protocol, a titration period, and verification by a sleep physician, and it works for a defined group of patients rather than everyone who snores. Patients in Willow Glen and the wider San José area who have a diagnosis and an unused machine are the clearest candidates, and the first appointment is an examination, not a fitting.
